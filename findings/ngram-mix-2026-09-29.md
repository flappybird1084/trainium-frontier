# Train-split n-gram model mixed into V86: offline measurement (2026-09-28)

**Verdict: POSITIVE. With parameters fitted on train only, the gain on val is -0.0085 bpb
(0.966782 -> 0.958320).** That uses exact counts over all 313M train tokens, context orders 2 to 16, and
Witten-Bell smoothing. The mixing weight lambda is looked up from three things: the highest matched order, log2 c(h),
and the top continuation's share. The val-fitted ceiling is -0.0087. A row bootstrap gives SE 0.0007
(5-95%: -0.0097 to -0.0074).

The version that can ship depends on table size. The table below lists the conservative (pruned) numbers. The gain is
still growing with data: -0.0030 at 25%, -0.0049 at 50% and -0.0085 at 100%. Nearly half the gain comes from
near-duplicate documents shared by train and val.

## Setup
- Val is the same 2048 rows (2,097,152 targets) as `cache-ceiling`. V86 log-probs come from `val.npz`, and the
  baseline reproduces 0.966782. bpb is computed exactly as `evaluate_bpb` does it.
- The fit set is the train holdout: the last 4 row groups of shard 4, 2048 rows packed the same way (`train.npz`).
  **These row groups are excluded from the counts.** The counts cover the other 312,743,013 train tokens, tokenized
  like the loaders (BOS + `encode_ordinary`).
- The n-gram context of each position uses only that row's earlier tokens. At row starts it falls back to lower orders.
- λ may depend only on context features and never on the target. Nothing is tuned on val. V86 saw the holdout once
  in training, so the train-fitted λ is slightly conservative. The train-fit to ceiling gap is only 0.0002.
- The counting is exact: sort and run-length encode, partitioned by the last context token. Orders 2 to 5 use exact
  63-bit keys (in `count.py`, checked against brute force). Orders 2 to 16 use a 51-bit context hash plus a 13-bit
  token, which agrees with exact counts on 99.99%+ of queries (`count2.py`).

## Per-order breakdown (all counts, WB, λ(max order, log2 c(h)); cumulative val gain, train-fit)

| max order | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 10 | 12 | 14 | 16 | 16 + top-share feature |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| val Δbpb | -0.00015 | -0.00052 | -0.00181 | -0.00318 | -0.00425 | -0.00513 | -0.00588 | -0.00699 | -0.00750 | -0.00769 | -0.00775 | **-0.00846** |

- Orders 2 and 3 add almost nothing, because the model already knows them. The gain starts at order 4 and keeps
  growing to 12. A global λ gives only half the gain (-0.0042), so evidence-dependent λ matters.
- Interpolated modified Kneser-Ney (exact, orders ≤5) gave -0.0028 with standard discounts. Scaling the discounts
  ×0.7 gave -0.0035. WB with β=2 (fitted on the holdout) gave -0.0032 at order 5. The mix prefers little smoothing.
- The logit-space (log-linear) mix was implemented as follows. For each order it adds a bias θ[order, count bin,
  share bin] to the logit of that order's top n-gram token, and renormalizes exactly (975 scalars, LBFGS on the
  holdout). Train-fit val gain: **-0.0070** (ceiling -0.0072). It needs only the top-1 token per context.
- Where the gain sits: 0.7% of val positions have an order-16 context seen in train, and they carry 47% of the gain.
  V86 scores 0.741 bpb on those positions and the mix scores 0.187. Top 2% of rows = 43% of the gain; top 10% =
  73%. They are near-duplicate web documents (news listings, USDA plant profiles, press releases). The other half of
  the gain is spread over common multi-word phrases at orders 4-8.

## Table size (the real constraint) and build time

The exact tables for orders 2-16 hold 3.5 billion contexts (tens of GB), so they cannot ship. The bounded variants
below are conservative: dropped mass is not redistributed. Each row was refit on the holdout.

| shippable variant | val Δbpb (train-fit) | size |
|---|---|---|
| log-linear top-1, orders 2-16, contexts c(h)≥5 | -0.0014 | 0.20 GB |
| log-linear top-1, orders 4,6,8,12,16, c(h)≥2 | -0.0029 | 0.53 GB |
| log-linear top-1, orders 3,4,5,6,8,10,12,16, c(h)≥2 | -0.0033 | 0.87 GB |
| linear WB, entries c≥5 at orders ≤4, c≥2 at 6,8,12,16 | -0.0027 | 0.80 GB |
| linear WB, entries c≥2, orders 2,3,4,5,6,8,10,12,16 | -0.0034 | 1.84 GB |
| log-linear top-1, 4,6,8,12 c≥2 + ALL contexts at 16 | -0.0053 | 2.99 GB |
| linear WB, entries c≥2 + ALL entries at 16 | -0.0049 | 6.2 GB |
| log-linear, ALL contexts at 4,5,6,7,8,10,12,14,16 | -0.0068 | 18 GB |

(Top-1 entry = 4 B fingerprint + 2 B token + 1-2 B count/share bin, taken as 8 B. A WB entry has 8 B and each of
its contexts another 8 B.)
- Singletons (contexts seen once) make up the duplicate-document half of the gain, and pruning at c≥2 removes them.
  A cheaper way to keep them is to ship the train token stream itself (626 MB uint16). Add an index of 16-gram hashes
  at every 8th position (39M x 8 B = 0.3 GB). At eval, find an anchor and follow the corpus pointer while the row
  keeps matching, like induction against the corpus. That is about 0.9 GB for most of the order-16 gain, but it is
  not measured.
- **Build time on this box (i9-class, 12 cores), counts only:**
  - Tokenizing the 5 shards takes 30 s on 12 threads.
  - Exact counting of orders 2-5 over 313M tokens takes 50 s single-threaded, peak 4.1 GB.
  - Hashed counting of orders 2-16 takes 144 s single-threaded (peak 6.6 GB) or 74 s with 4 processes. This
    includes the query lookups, and scaling is memory-bound.
  - Counting is split into 8 independent partitions (by the last context token), so it maps one to one onto the 8
    torchrun ranks. Each rank would count its own partition in a background thread (numpy sort releases the GIL)
    in about 20-30 s.
  - With the ~124 GB host RAM, memory is no issue.

## Feasibility sketch (gain ≥ 0.002, so it is written out)
1. **Build during training.** In a background thread, each rank tokenizes the shards (or reuses the loader's
   tokens), counts partition r of 8, prunes, and turns the result into a fixed-size open-addressing hash table:
   fingerprint, top token, count bin, share bin. It is done within about 1-2 minutes, on host CPU that is otherwise
   idle. The tables are gathered into the consolidated checkpoint as int tensors.
2. **Which mixing form.**
   - **Log-linear top-1 is the recommended form.** The forward adds a bias θ_n to the logit of each order's top
     token: at most ~8-15 scalar adds per position, as a sparse scatter into the (T, V) logits.
   - The fused head/CE kernel needs only a per-row correction to the log-sum-exp:
     lse' = lse + log(1 + Σ_distinct p(τ)(e^B - 1)), which needs z at ≤15 gathered columns. In the backward pass,
     p' = p/Zcorr everywhere, and ≤15 columns get an extra e^B factor, so the fix-up can happen after the kernel.
   - Linear mixing is -0.0015 better with full tables. But eval must return dense log((1-λ)softmax(z) + λq), which
     needs the full continuation list of every context (CSR tables, much larger). Its training loss is cheap,
     though: the gradient is the plain CE gradient scaled per position by r = (1-λ)p_y / ((1-λ)p_y + λq_y).
3. **Train/eval parity.**
   - Apply the same bias in the training forward, and make θ (~1000 scalars) trainable parameters.
   - The counts must then be **leave-one-out**, meaning the row's own occurrence is removed:
     c(h,y)-1, c(h)-1, and a singleton context becomes unseen. Without this, every training row finds itself in the
     table and the model learns to trust it blindly.
   - With leave-one-out, the logit-path code is identical in training and eval. The only difference is that eval
     rows are simply absent from the table, which is the same condition leave-one-out creates.
   - The alternative, a fixed mixture fitted post-hoc on a train holdout at the end of the run and applied only at
     eval, is exactly what was measured here. It does break "same logit path", so the reviewer would likely reject it.
   - Training jointly might gain more than post-hoc, because the network stops spending capacity on memorized
     phrases, or less. That is unmeasured.
4. **Eval forward.**
   - Rolling context hashes use int64 wraparound multiplies, which is risky on Neuron. Two options: 2x int32 hashing
     on device plus gathers into an HBM hash table with a fixed probe count, or moving the 1024 row tokens to host,
     looking them up in numpy, and returning (T, 15) indices and biases.
   - The forward stays a pure causal function of the row's tokens plus fixed checkpoint data.
   - Eval cost is about 15 lookups per token.
5. **Risks.**
   - About half the gain is from duplicated documents. The private eval shards come from the same shuffled
     ClimbMix pool, so a similar rate is expected, but it is a distributional bet.
   - A reviewer may view a multi-GB lookup table as "memorization", though it is built only from train data during
     the run.
   - Checkpoint size limits are unverified: V86.pt is already 1.4 GB.
   - Joint training with leave-one-out is unmeasured.
